1. Introduction / Theory

Electroencephalography (EEG) measures electrical activity from the scalp using electrodes. The measured EEG is a time-varying electrical signal that can be digitised and processed.

This Virtual Lab uses prerecorded/sample EEG data because physical EEG acquisition hardware is not available. It does not measure the user's brain activity.
This simulation is for educational purposes and must not be used for medical diagnosis.

How should my CSV file look?
  • One column of amplitude values in microvolts (µV), one sample per row, or two columns: time in seconds, then amplitude.
  • Lines starting with # are comments. A line such as # fs=256 sets the sampling frequency.
  • If the file has no fs line and no time column, the value typed above is used. At least 64 samples are needed.
  • Sampling frequency means how many samples were recorded per second. Typical EEG: 128, 256 or 512 Hz. A wrong value shifts every frequency in the results.

5. FFT / Frequency-Domain Analysis

Next step: after loading the signal, click Perform FFT to see which frequencies it contains.
Why do we need the Fourier Transform?

The EEG waveform in section 3 looks like a messy wiggle because it is many brain rhythms added together. By eye you cannot tell how much of it is slow (delta) or fast (beta). The Fourier Transform rewrites the signal as a sum of simple sine waves and tells you the strength of each frequency.

X[k] = Σ x[n] e−j2πkn/N,   frequency of bin k = k · fs / N

  • Find the dominant rhythm: a tall peak near 10 Hz means strong alpha activity.
  • Spot noise: a sharp peak at 50 Hz is mains hum, and broad high-frequency content is usually muscle noise.
  • Choose filter cutoffs sensibly (section 7).
  • Measure band energy (section 11) and detect abnormal slowing (section 12).
  • Why the FFT and not the plain DFT? For N = 4096 samples the DFT needs about 16.7 million multiplications. The FFT needs about N·log2N = 49,000, roughly 340 times fewer.

How to read the graph: x-axis = frequency in Hz, y-axis = how strong that frequency is (µV). Taller peak = stronger rhythm. This lab removes the average value, applies a Hann window and zero-pads to a power of two before the FFT.

6. EEG Frequency Bands

Why are there frequency bands, and what do they indicate?

Brain cells fire in groups, and groups that fire together create rhythmic voltage waves. Different brain states (deep sleep, relaxed rest, concentration) produce rhythms in different frequency ranges. Early researchers, starting with Hans Berger in the 1920s, named these rhythms with Greek letters. Splitting the spectrum into bands turns a complicated signal into a few numbers that can be compared between people, states and time.

BandFrequencyUsually linked with (healthy)When it may be abnormal
Delta0.5–4 HzDeep sleep in adults; normal and strong in infants.Dominant in an awake adult (diffuse slowing), or slow waves confined to one area (focal slowing). Can relate to brain injury, tumour, stroke, encephalopathy or sedatives.
Theta4–8 HzDrowsiness, light sleep, daydreaming, memory processing; common in children.Excess theta in an awake adult suggests slowing. Seen after head injury, in some dementias and with certain drugs.
Alpha8–13 HzRelaxed wakefulness with eyes closed; strongest at the back of the head; fades when eyes open.Missing, much weaker on one side, or slowed below 8 Hz in an awake adult.
Beta13–30 HzAlert thinking, focus, active concentration.Excess diffuse beta is typical of sedative drugs such as benzodiazepines. Muscle tension also creates fast activity that mimics it.
Gamma>30 HzHigher-level processing and combining information; mainly studied in research.On the scalp it is mostly muscle or electrical noise, so it is hard to interpret clinically.

Commonly used approximate ranges; boundaries vary by convention. Which band is normal depends on age, whether the person is awake or asleep, eyes open or closed, and which electrode is used. A band on its own is not a diagnosis; specialists look at patterns across the whole recording.

7. EEG Digital Filtering

Next step: pick a band below (for example Alpha), then click Apply Filter.
What is digital filtering and why is it needed?

A digital filter is a small algorithm that works on the sampled numbers x[n] and produces a new signal y[n]. It passes the frequencies you want and blocks the ones you do not.

  • EEG is tiny and noisy. It is only microvolts, so it is easily polluted: slow electrode drift (<0.5 Hz), eye blinks (about 0.5–4 Hz), muscle activity (>30 Hz) and mains hum (50 Hz in India).
  • To study one rhythm. A band-pass filter keeps only, say, alpha (8–13 Hz) so you can look at that rhythm alone.
  • To make later analysis reliable. Removing noise first stops it from distorting band energy and abnormality checks.
TypeKeepsTypical EEG use
Low-passBelow the cutoffRemove muscle noise and high-frequency hiss.
High-passAbove the cutoffRemove slow baseline drift (for example above 0.5 Hz).
Band-passBetween two cutoffsIsolate one band such as alpha or beta.
Notch (not in this lab)Everything except a narrow gapRemove 50 Hz mains hum.

Implementation: Butterworth-type biquad cascades (4th order per edge), applied forward and backward (zero phase). y[n] = H{x[n]}. Gamma is set to 30–80 Hz.

11. Signal Energy Calculation

How do we calculate the energy of an EEG signal?

Energy measures how strong the signal is overall: bigger swings mean more energy. For a sampled signal, square every sample (so negatives count too), add them up, and multiply by the sampling interval.

E = Σ x²[n] · Ts   (µV²·s)

Average power P = E / T = (1/N) Σ x²[n]   |   RMS = √P

  • Energy in a band: by Parseval's theorem, energy in time equals energy in frequency, so you can add up |X[k]|² only over the FFT bins inside a band, for example 8–13 Hz for alpha.
  • Relative energy (%) = band energy ÷ total energy. It shows which rhythm dominates, whatever the recording's overall amplitude.
  • The average (DC) value is removed first, as in the FFT.

12. Detecting Faults in the Brain from the Signal

Important: this is a learning tool. A single recording or a few numbers cannot diagnose a brain disorder. Doctors (neurologists) read multi-channel EEG, compare left with right, and consider age, medicines and symptoms. Never use this page to judge your own or anyone's health.

What do specialists look for?

Pattern in the signalHow it looksWhy it matters
Excess slow waves while awakeLarge, slow waves. In the spectrum, most power sits at 0.5–8 Hz.Normal in sleep or drowsiness. Persistent slowing in an awake adult can point to widespread brain dysfunction. Slowing in one region can point to a local problem such as injury or stroke.
Missing or weak alphaNo clear peak at 8–13 Hz when the eyes are closed and relaxed.Can be normal in some people. It can also accompany brain dysfunction or a poor recording.
Spikes and sharp wavesSudden, narrow, high-amplitude transients that stand out from the background.Associated with a tendency towards epileptic seizures.
Rhythmic, high-amplitude burstsRegular repeating waves, for example the 3 Hz spike-and-wave pattern, that start and stop abruptly.Typical of seizure activity.
Left–right asymmetryOne side is lower in amplitude or slower than the other (needs several channels).May suggest a one-sided problem.
Very flat, very low amplitudeAlmost no voltage variation.Can mean severe suppression of brain activity, but is far more often a loose or disconnected electrode.

Rule out artifacts first

Many "abnormal-looking" signals are not from the brain at all:

  • Eye blinks: large slow bumps, strongest at the front of the head (look like delta).
  • Muscle activity (jaw clench, frowning): broad, fast, noisy content above 20–30 Hz.
  • Mains hum: a sharp peak at 50 Hz (60 Hz in some countries).
  • Electrode problems: sudden jumps, drift or a flat line.

The signal-processing workflow is therefore: 1. clean the signal with filters, 2. run the FFT, 3. measure band energy, 4. compare with what is expected for that state, 5. have a specialist review any unusual pattern.
